INT. ST. PAUL'S METHODIST CHURCH - FELLOWSHIP HALL - DAY

Low ceiling. Folding tables under paper tablecloths. A crockpot
of meatballs, a spiral ham, three competing banana puddings.
Forty people in their good dark clothes, talking at the volume
of people who just came from somewhere quiet.

NORA CASTELLANO (38) stands near the exit in a black suit that
cost more than the church kitchen. Phone in hand, screen dark.
She holds it like a reason to leave.

BEV TURNER (70s), cardigan, reading glasses on a beaded chain,
presses a paper plate on her: a pimento cheese sandwich, cut in
triangles, crusts off.

                    BEV
          Your mother made the pimento cheese
          for every funeral in this church for
          thirty years. Somebody had to make it
          for hers.

                    NORA
          Thank you, Mrs. Turner.

                    BEV
          It's not as good as hers. Don't you
          tell me it is.

Bev squeezes her arm and moves on. Nora looks at the sandwich.
Sets the plate down on the nearest table.

Across the hall, at the coffee station, a MAN is down on one
knee beside the big steel urn, working at the spigot with a
butter knife. Late sixties. Jacket off and folded over a chair.
Hands that have fixed a lot of things.

RAY CASTELLANO.

Nora sees him. Everything in her goes still.

Ray stands. Tries the spigot. Coffee runs clean. He gives it a
small nod, satisfied -- then looks up and finds her.

A long moment across the room. Nobody else notices.

Ray fills two styrofoam cups. Walks over. Holds one out.

                    RAY
          Gasket sticks if they fill it past
          the line. Every year somebody fills
          it past the line.

She doesn't take it. He doesn't lower it. Finally she takes it,
mostly to make it stop.

                    NORA
          Thank you.

                    RAY
          You look like her. Around the eyes.
          I bet people keep telling you that.

                    NORA
          Four people. In the receiving line.

                    RAY
          Well. Five.

Nora sets the coffee down beside the sandwich. Untouched.

                    NORA
          I didn't see you at the service.

                    RAY
          I was in the back. By the radiator.
                    (beat)
          Didn't figure you'd want me up front.

                    NORA
          You could have sat wherever you
          wanted.

                    RAY
          Could I.

She lets that go by.

                    RAY (CONT'D)
          How's New York?

                    NORA
          Fine. Busy.

                    RAY
          She said you made partner. Said they
          gave you the corner office and you
          turned the desk to face the wall so
          you wouldn't spend all day looking
          out the window.

Nora's eyes come up to his. That is not a thing you hear
secondhand at the hardware store.

                    NORA
          When did she tell you that?

                    RAY
          She was proud of it. She told
          everybody.

It's not an answer. She knows it's not an answer.

                    NORA
          Dad --
                    (catches it)
          Ray. If this is about the estate,
          there's a process. Have your attorney
          contact my office and we can --

                    RAY
          I don't have an attorney. I had one,
          once. Fella from Dayton with a
          clip-on tie.
                    (beat)
          You ate him alive. I never told you
          that. It was something to watch.

                    NORA
          You weren't supposed to tell me. You
          were the other side.

                    RAY
          I know which side I was.

Somewhere behind them, Bev laughs at something. Too loud, then
remembers where she is.

                    NORA
          How's Denise?

                    RAY
          Denise moved to Sarasota in 2017.

                    NORA
          I'm sorry.

                    RAY
          No, you're not.

                    NORA
          No. I'm not.

Something on him that is almost a smile. He reaches into his
pants pocket and comes out with a ring of keys. A Buick fob. A
brass house key. A Kroger loyalty tag, worn white at the edges.

                    RAY
          These are hers. The car's at my place.

                    NORA
          Why is Mom's car at your place?

Ray takes his time.

                    RAY
          Columbus is an hour and ten each way.
          She wasn't supposed to drive herself
          home after.

                    NORA
          Carol drove her. Carol Hensley. She
          told me Carol --

                    RAY
          Carol's eighty-one. Carol doesn't do
          the interstate.

Nora stares at him.

                    NORA
          How long?

                    RAY
          Since February. Tuesdays and Fridays.
          Some days she'd want a Frosty after,
          at the Wendy's off 70. She'd eat half
          and make me hold the rest the whole
          way home.

                    NORA
          I talked to her every Sunday. Every
          single Sunday. She never once --

                    RAY
          I know.

                    NORA
          Why wouldn't she tell me?

Ray looks at her a long time. Deciding whether to say it.

                    RAY
          She said if you knew it was me, you'd
          stop picking up on Sundays.

Nora's face doesn't move. That's how we know it landed.

                    NORA
                    (quiet)
          Would I have?

                    RAY
          I don't know, Nora. I don't know you
          well enough to say.

He says it plainly. Not as a weapon. It would be easier if it
were.

He sets the keys on the table, next to the sandwich.

                    RAY (CONT'D)
          It pulls left. I kept meaning to get
          under it.
                    (beat)
          Eat that. Bev's watching.

He crosses back to the chair, shrugs into his jacket, and goes
out the side door into the parking lot. He doesn't look back.

Nora stands alone in the noise.

Her phone buzzes in her hand. The screen: DAVID KESSLER -
HALVORSEN CLOSING. She watches it ring. Turns it face-down on
the paper tablecloth.

She picks up the keys. Turns the ring over. On the brass house
key, a strip of masking tape, and in her mother's handwriting,
faded Sharpie:

RAY'S - SPARE.

Nora looks up. Through the window, Ray's pickup backs out of
its space, slow, and signals left onto Main -- though there is
no one on the road to see it.

She sits down for the first time all day. Picks up a triangle
of sandwich. Takes a bite.

It's not as good as hers.

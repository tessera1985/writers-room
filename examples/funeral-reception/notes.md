# Notes on "Funeral Reception"

*The scene in `scene.txt` was written by Claude from a one-line prompt: a lawyer comes home for her mother's funeral and runs into the father she hasn't spoken to in ten years. A separate Claude session, given only the `scene-audit` skill, wrote the notes below. It wasn't told the scene was AI-written.*

---

At her mother's funeral lunch, Nora meets her estranged father and learns he's been driving her mother to treatment for months, which her mother hid from her.

**INTENTION AND OBSTACLE**
- **Nora:** wants to get through this and leave without dealing with Ray. Then, once the desk detail tips her off, she wants to know what went on between him and her mother. In the way: Ray, who hands her the truth slowly, and her own refusal to go soft on him. ✅
- **Ray:** wants to give her the keys and, under that, have her know what he did for her mother. In the way: Nora's coldness, and his own habit of saying less than he means. "Ray takes his time" plays it well. ✅

**NOTES**

1. **"RAY'S - SPARE."**
   Designed, not discovered: the payoffs stack up at the end. Almost every setup in the scene comes back. The phone she "holds like a reason to leave" gets turned face-down. The plate she set down becomes the bite. Bev's "Don't you tell me it is" returns as "It's not as good as hers." "Hands that have fixed a lot of things" leads to "It pulls left," which leads to the truck signaling left. And the key gets its tape. That's five payoffs in about fifteen lines after Ray leaves, and they compete with the best one. Which of them could the scene live without?

2. **"signals left onto Main -- though there is no one on the road to see it."**
   Designed and narrated at once. The audience can't see "no one to see it." That clause exists to explain the symbol. It also lines up too neatly with "It pulls left" a page earlier.

3. **"He says it plainly. Not as a weapon. It would be easier if it were."**
   Narrated feeling, and this one fails the strip test. "I don't know you well enough to say" is the line the scene turns on, and it can be played as a jab. Right now only the reader is told it isn't one. What does Ray do on the page that tells the audience how he means it?

4. **"You ate him alive. I never told you that. It was something to watch."**
   Lost me, a little. Was Nora her mother's lawyer in the divorce? "You were the other side" suggests it, but a first-time reader may stop to work it out, and it matters because it explains why Ray sat by the radiator. If you're holding it back on purpose, can the reader tell?

5. **"That is not a thing you hear secondhand at the hardware store."**
   Style note only. This line, along with "It's not an answer. She knows it's not an answer." and "That's how we know it landed.", sits on top of behavior that already plays without it. Strip test passes. Keep them or cut them as you like.

**WORKING**
"Dad -- (catches it) Ray." and "Could I." both trust the audience completely. So does the turned-around desk, the detail that tells Nora her mother and Ray were close before anyone says it. Protect all three.

**COULD END**
After "RAY'S - SPARE." The tape in her mother's handwriting changes the meaning of everything Ray just said, and it's a discovery she makes, not one we're handed. Everything after it is a payoff waiting its turn.
